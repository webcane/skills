# Difficult Conversations

> "Difficult conversations are almost never about getting the facts right. They are about conflicting perceptions, interpretations, and values." — Douglas Stone

## The Three Conversations Model

Every difficult conversation is actually **three conversations** happening simultaneously. Most people only address the surface. Mastery requires navigating all three.

```
+--------------------------------------------------+
|           THE DIFFICULT CONVERSATION              |
|                                                   |
|  1. "What Happened?" Conversation      |
|     Truth → Contribution                          |
|                                                   |
|  2. The Feelings Conversation           |
|     Suppression → Acknowledgment                  |
|                                                   |
|  3. The Identity Conversation          |
|     All-or-Nothing → Complexity                   |
+--------------------------------------------------+
```

---

## Conversation 1: "What Happened?"

### The Trap: The Truth Assumption

We assume: "I am right, they are wrong." Both sides believe this.

### The Shift: From Truth to Contribution

Instead of asking "Who's right?", ask "What did each of us **contribute** to this situation?"

### Three Sub-shifts:

1. **Stop arguing about who's right** — Move from certainty to curiosity
2. **Don't assume intent** — Impact ≠ Intent. They may have had good intentions and still caused harm.
3. **Map contributions, not blame** — Blame is backward-looking and shuts down learning. Contribution is forward-looking.

---

## Conversation 2: The Feelings Conversation

### The Trap: Feelings Are Irrelevant

We think: "This is a work issue, feelings don't belong." But unexpressed feelings **leak** — as sarcasm, withdrawal, rigidity, or aggression.

### The Shift: Acknowledge Before Problem-Solving

**Feelings are not obstacles to the conversation — they ARE the conversation.**

Steps:
1. **Name your feelings to yourself** — "I feel unappreciated" is more useful than "I'm fine"
2. **Negotiate with your feelings** — Are they based on assumptions? Incomplete information?
3. **Share your feelings without blame** — "I felt hurt" not "You hurt me"
4. **Acknowledge their feelings** — Before you can be heard, they need to feel heard

### The Feelings Inventory

Common hidden feelings in workplace conflicts:
- **Unappreciated** — "My work doesn't matter to them"
- **Incompetent** — "Maybe I'm not good enough"
- **Disrespected** — "They don't value my perspective"
- **Afraid** — "What if I lose my job/reputation?"
- **Guilty** — "I know I contributed to this but can't admit it"

## Conversation 3: The Identity Conversation

### The Trap: All-or-Nothing Thinking

Every difficult conversation threatens our self-image. Three core identity questions:

1. **Am I competent?** — Or am I actually incompetent?
2. **Am I a good person?** — Or am I actually selfish/unfair?
3. **Am I worthy of love/respect?** — Or will they reject me?

### The Shift: Embrace Complexity

The truth is never all-or-nothing:
- "I am **both** competent AND I made a mistake"
- "I am **both** a caring manager AND I handled this poorly"
- "I am **both** valuable AND there's room to grow"

### Ground Your Identity Before the Conversation

Before entering the conversation:
1. Identify which identity issue is at stake for you
2. Accept that you are complex (both/and, not either/or)
3. Let go of controlling their reaction — you can't
4. Prepare for the worst-case identity threat and pre-accept it

## The Learning Conversation

The goal of a difficult conversation is NOT to deliver a message. It IS to **learn**.

### The Third Story

Start from the perspective of a neutral observer — someone who has no stake in the outcome.

| Your Story | Their Story | Third Story |
|------------|-------------|-------------|
| "You missed the deadline and don't care" | "The requirements changed and nobody told me" | "There seems to be a gap between expectations and what was communicated about the timeline" |

The Third Story:
- Describes the **difference** between you, not who's right
- Uses neutral language
- Invites both perspectives
- Does NOT take sides

### Opening Lines Using the Third Story

**Bad openings**:
- "We need to talk about your performance." (accusation)
- "I'm sure this will be fine, but..." (minimizing)
- "Don't take this the wrong way..." (priming defensiveness)

**Good openings**:
- "I'd like to talk about [topic]. I think we see it differently, and I'd like to understand your perspective."
- "I've been thinking about [situation] and I realize I might be missing something. Can we talk?"
- "There's something that's been on my mind. I'm not sure I'm seeing it right, but I want to share it and hear your thoughts."

## Safety Warning: When NOT to Use This Framework

This framework assumes a **roughly equal power dynamic** between parties. Do NOT use in:
- **Domestic violence or abuse** — "finding your contribution" can blame the victim
- **Harassment situations** — the perpetrator is responsible, not the target
- **Active addiction/crisis** — professional help needed first

## Process: Preparing for a Difficult Conversation

### Step 1: Sort Out the Three Conversations

Write down:
- **What Happened**: What's my story? What might their story be? What did we each contribute?
- **Feelings**: What am I feeling? What might they be feeling?
- **Identity**: What's at stake for my self-image? What's at stake for theirs?

### Step 2: Decide Whether to Raise It

Ask:
- Is this really about them, or is it about me?
- Is there a simpler way to address this?
- What's my purpose? To change them, or to understand?

### Step 3: Start from the Third Story

- Describe the gap or difference
- Invite their perspective first
- Share yours as a perspective, not the truth

### Step 4: Explore Their Story

- Ask and listen
- Paraphrase to confirm understanding
- Acknowledge their feelings before asserting yours

### Step 5: Problem-Solve Together

Only after both stories are heard:
- Invent options that address both sides' concerns
- Agree on next steps
- Establish how to handle it if the issue recurs

## Anti-patterns

1. **Leading with Your Conclusion** — "You need to do X" skips the learning. Start with curiosity.

2. **Assuming Intent from Impact** — "You did X, so you must have intended to hurt me." Impact and intent are independent.

3. **The Hit-and-Run** — Dropping the bomb and leaving. Difficult conversations require staying present.

4. **Binary Thinking About Identity** — "If I made a mistake, I'm a failure." Replace with "I made a mistake AND I'm still competent."

5. **Fixing Before Feeling** — Jumping to solutions before acknowledging emotions guarantees the conversation will repeat.

6. **The Blame Frame** — "Whose fault is it?" Replace with "What did we each contribute?"

7. **Rehearsing Your Monologue** — Preparing what to SAY instead of preparing what to ASK.

8. **Avoiding the Conversation Entirely** — The cost of avoidance compounds. Unspoken issues don't resolve; they metastasize.
