# Analogy — Transfer from Another Domain

> Take a solved problem from a distant domain and map it onto yours. The
> structural similarity — not the surface similarity — is what carries
> the solution across.

## Origin

Central to how experts reason: solutions transfer between domains
through analogy. Widely documented in cognitive science and used
systematically in engineering (e.g., TRIZ's cross-industry patterns).

## Application to Problem Analysis

When a problem feels stuck, analogy supplies a source domain whose
solutions can be mapped onto the problem.

### 1. Name the Problem

State the essence of the problem without domain jargon.

> "Improve the recommendation system of an online cinema."

### 2. Choose a Distant Source Domain

Pick a domain that faces the same structural challenge; the farther the
better.

> A head chef in a restaurant. The chef does not just offer a menu — he
> reads the guest's mood and offers a tasting set of unexpected
> combinations.

### 3. Map the Analogy

Transfer the source's mechanism onto the problem.

> The recommendation system should offer not just similar films but
> "thematic sets" — e.g., "films for a rainy evening" — to surprise and
> engage the user.

### 4. Extract the Hypothesis

State the transferred idea as a testable hypothesis.

> "Offering curated thematic sets will engage users more than offering
> similar films alone."

## When to Use

A problem with no obvious next step, a solution that has not been found
inside the domain, or a desire to generate novel alternatives rather
than refine the current one.

## Output

The problem restated, the source domain chosen and why, the mapped
analogy, and the hypothesis it generates.
