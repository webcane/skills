# Three Questions — Why? What? How?

> Before reacting, pause and analyze from three positions: the cause
> ("Why?"), the current facts ("What?"), and the constructive response
> ("How?"). This converts an automatic reaction into a conscious action.

## Origin

From Daniel J. Siegel and Tina Payne Bryson's work on relationships and
parenting (*The Whole-Brain Child*, *The Power of Showing Up*): the
discipline to pause, seek the motive, see the facts, and choose a
response that solves and teaches.

## Application to Problem Analysis

When a problem provokes an emotional or automatic reaction, the three
questions interrupt the reaction and replace it with analysis.

### 1. Why?

What is the cause of the behavior or event? Seek the motive with
empathy rather than judgment. ("Why is the user leaving?")

### 2. What?

What is objectively happening right now? Strip out interpretation and
state only the facts. ("What exactly does the user do before leaving?")

### 3. How?

How should we respond so that we solve the problem and, where people
are involved, teach rather than punish? ("How can we change the
experience?")

### For Hypotheses

The method converts a vague "why" into an observable "what" and an
actionable "how":

> "Why does the user leave?" → "What does he do right before leaving?"
> → "How can we alter that moment?"

## When to Use

Emotional reactions to a problem, communication breakdowns, "people
problems", or any situation where the first response would be reactive
rather than analytical.

## Output

The motive (Why), the objective facts (What), and the constructive
response (How) — plus the sharper hypothesis that emerges when the
question is pushed from "why" to "what" to "how".
