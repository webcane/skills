# Deduction — From General to Specific

> "All men are mortal; Socrates is a man; therefore Socrates is mortal."
> Deduction moves from a general premise to a specific conclusion. When
> the premises are true, the conclusion is guaranteed — but the method
> never adds new knowledge about the world. It unpacks what the premises
> already contain.

## Origin

Formalized by Aristotle and sharpened by Descartes; popularized in
detective fiction through Sherlock Holmes. Deduction is the logic of
certainty given fixed premises.

## Application to Problem Analysis

When a proposal claims to follow from accepted principles, deduction
tests whether the conclusion actually follows — and surfaces which
premises the conclusion secretly depends on.

### 1. State the Premises

Write down the general rules or accepted facts the proposal rests on.

### 2. Derive the Consequences

Work step by step from the premises to the specific conclusion. Each
step must follow from the previous one by a rule you can name.

### 3. Test Each Link

- Is each premise true as stated?
- Does the conclusion follow from the stated premises, or was an
  unstated premise smuggled in?
- Would the same reasoning hold with different examples?

### 4. Label What You Learned

The conclusion is guaranteed only if the premises hold. It contains no
new information about the world — it re-exposes what was already
assumed. If the result feels surprising, an unstated assumption is
probably doing the work.

## When to Use

Claims of the form "this follows from our stated principles", "by
definition", "if X then necessarily Y" — anywhere certainty is claimed
from general rules.

## Output

The stated premises, the derived consequences, any smuggled-in
premises exposed, and a verdict on whether the conclusion really
follows.
