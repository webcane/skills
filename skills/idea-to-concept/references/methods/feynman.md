# Feynman Technique — Learning Through Teaching

> If Socrates interrogates others to test their ideas, the Feynman
> Technique has you explain a concept to someone else to test your own.
> Take a complex concept and explain it so plainly that an
> eighth-grader could follow it. Wherever you get stuck, you have found
> a gap in your own understanding — go back, fill it, and repeat.

## Origin

Popularized through Richard Feynman's name: if you cannot explain
something simply, you do not yet understand it. The technique is a
self-verification loop — teach to find out what you do not know.

## Application to Problem Analysis

When a problem or proposal relies on a concept you are expected to
already understand, the Feynman Technique checks whether that
understanding is real or borrowed.

### 1. Explain in Plain Language

Explain the concept as if to an eighth-grader. No jargon, no
hand-waving — only simple words and concrete examples.

### 2. Find the Gaps

The moment you cannot find a simple analogy, cannot finish a sentence
without a technical term, or start hand-waving, you have found a gap in
your own understanding. Name it precisely.

### 3. Go Back and Fill the Gap

Return to the source material, close the gap, and repeat the
explanation from the start. Repeat until the explanation survives
without gaps.

### 4. Verify, Don't Discover

This method verifies existing understanding — it does not generate new
knowledge. If a concept passes, it means you understand what is already
there; it says nothing about whether the concept is right.

## When to Use

Depth-of-understanding checks: "do I actually understand this?", a
proposal that depends on a concept nobody has explained, a claim that
"everyone knows how this works".

## Output

The gap-free plain-language explanation, the list of gaps found and
filled, and a verdict on whether the concept was understood or merely
recognized.
