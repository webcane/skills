# Inversion — Thinking Upside Down

> Instead of asking "How do I succeed?", ask "How would I guarantee
> failure?" — then avoid it. Inverting a hypothesis reveals the risks it
> hides and often generates a better hypothesis by negating the obvious
> one.

## Origin

Used deliberately by Charlie Munger ("invert, always invert", borrowed
from Carl Jacobi) and by engineers and mathematicians wherever the
negative formulation is easier to reason about than the positive.

## Application to Problem Analysis

When a hypothesis states what to do, inversion tests what happens if
the assumption is pushed to its destructive extreme.

### 1. State the Hypothesis

> "To raise engagement, we should add more features."

### 2. Invert It

Ask how to guarantee the opposite outcome.

> "To kill engagement, add more features and confuse the user."

### 3. Read the Inverted Result

The inverted statement immediately highlights what the original
hypothesis ignores — here, the complexity risk.

### 4. Derive the Better Hypothesis

Negate the failure mode into a constructive claim.

> "Simplifying the interface will raise engagement more than adding
> features."

## When to Use

A hypothesis that only considers one direction ("more X = better"),
a plan that assumes success, or a problem where the failure modes are
easier to name than the success factors.

## Output

The original hypothesis, its inverted form, the risks the inversion
exposes, and the improved hypothesis derived from negating the failure
mode.
