# Mind Map — Visualizing Connections

> Place the central idea in the middle and let branches radiate outward
> with key concepts, associations, and questions. A mind map makes
> non-linear relationships visible that a linear text hides.

## Origin

Popularized by Tony Buzan in the 1970s as a note-taking and thinking
tool. Its value for problem analysis is structural: it externalizes a
web of associations around a central idea.

## Application to Problem Analysis

When a hypothesis is complex, a mind map decomposes it and reveals
connections and research points that linear reasoning misses.

### 1. Center the Idea

Write the hypothesis or problem in the center.

### 2. Radiate the Branches

Draw branches for each major component, and sub-branches for
associations, constraints, evidence, and open questions. Add freely —
capture everything, filter later.

### 3. Look for Non-Linear Links

Cross-link branches. A connection between two distant branches is often
a new hypothesis or a hidden assumption.

### 4. Extract Research Points

Mark the branches that need evidence. These are the points where the
hypothesis can be tested or refined.

## When to Use

A complex or tangled hypothesis, "sorting out" a messy idea, or a
desire to see the whole structure before deciding what to test.

## Output

The complete map, the non-linear connections found, and the list of
research points extracted from it.
